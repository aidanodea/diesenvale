A better way to
cost fabrication jobs.

Most people costing fabrication jobs do it from experience and instinct. That works until it doesn't — a job comes in thin, a quote goes out wrong, and you've already committed. There's a straightforward fix.

The problem

The knowledge is there.
The system isn't.

Quoting by gut feel works well on the jobs you've done a hundred times. You know the steel cost, you know roughly how long it'll take, you build in a bit of room and you're close enough. But that same approach applied to a complex or unfamiliar job — one with multiple operations, unusual materials, tight tolerances — is where things go wrong. Not obviously wrong. Quietly wrong, in ways you only notice when the job's finished and the numbers don't stack.

The real cost isn't a single bad quote. It's the compounding effect: margins that drift because there's no consistent model behind them. Jobs won at prices that don't make money. Jobs lost because the quote was too cautious. Hours spent on each one from scratch because there's no structure to pull from.

Whoever's doing the quoting has everything needed to cost jobs well — material knowledge, labour intuition, a clear sense of what work is worth. The problem is that knowledge sits in one person's head, applied differently every time, with no way to make it consistent or fast. That's the gap.

The fix

What an agent actually does.

The idea is straightforward: build a system that takes any job description and produces a structured cost estimate — using your data, your rates, your margins. Not a generic template. Not a guess. Your actual numbers, applied consistently every time.

1
You describe the job

Document, email, rough notes — anything. The agent reads it and extracts what matters: materials, operations, complexity, scope.

2
It pulls your rates

Materials costs, labour rates by role, machine time, overhead allocation — all drawn from a structured reference you've built and maintain.

3
It applies your margins

Target margin by job type, contingency buffer, subcontractor uplift — configured to match how you actually want to price work.

4
You review and send

A complete, structured quote lands in your hands. You check it, adjust where needed, and send it — in a fraction of the usual time.

The agent doesn't replace your knowledge — it applies it consistently. You're still the one who set the rates, defined the margins, and decides what goes out.
What needs to be in place

Before anything gets built.

The agent is only as good as the data behind it. Before building anything, this information needs to exist in a clean, structured format — not in your head, not scattered across old quotes and spreadsheets. Getting it out and organised is the first piece of work, and probably the most valuable.

  • Price list per material type (mild steel, stainless, aluminium, etc.), grade, and form (sheet, tube, bar, plate) — with units (kg, metre, sheet)
  • The stock sizes you actually buy, not theoretical catalogue sizes
  • Waste and yield factors per material type and cut process — how much scrap is typical?
  • Consumables with unit costs: welding wire, gas, grinding discs, abrasives, cutting fluid, paint, primer, fixings
  • Supplier price lists — ideally current, one primary supplier per material category
  • Hourly cost per role — welder, fabricator, machinist, finisher, labourer — true cost including employment on-costs, not the charge-out rate
  • Time estimates per operation type: cutting, bending, welding, grinding, finishing — per unit, metre, or joint
  • Setup time vs. run time — distinguished by machine or process
  • Shift patterns and available productive hours per week
  • Machine hourly rates: laser cutter, press brake, MIG/TIG welders, grinders, other equipment — depreciation plus maintenance plus energy per hour
  • Shop overhead rate: rent, insurance, utilities, admin — expressed as €/hour of productive time or as a percentage on top of direct costs
  • Subcontractor rates for anything sent out: galvanising, CNC, specialist coating, etc.
  • Target margin by job type — this likely differs between one-off fabrication, repeat production runs, and installation work
  • Contingency buffer policy — does a complex job carry 10%? Does a repeat job carry 5%? How do you currently decide?
  • Discount authority: what can be given away and how much, before it erodes the business
  • Payment terms assumptions — relevant for cash flow costing on larger jobs
  • Past quotes — even rough ones. These are the single best source for calibrating time estimates
  • Actuals vs. estimates: where did jobs overrun? Where were they quicker than expected? This is where the agent gets smarter over time
  • A simple job taxonomy — what categories of work do you actually do? (e.g. structural steel, architectural metalwork, bespoke fabrication, maintenance and repair)
  • Standard terms and exclusions — what's always left out of a quote (delivery, design, site work, etc.)
  • How you want quotes to look when they go out — level of detail, line item breakdown, presentation format
  • Any standard wording you always use around validity periods, payment terms, or variations

You probably don't have all of this yet. That's normal. Most of it lives in your head and in old jobs. Working through it together is part of the process, and doing it properly is what makes the agent actually useful.

The process

How this gets built.

Two phases. No unnecessary complexity.

Phase 1 Figure out what's there

Sit down and go through everything. What data exists, what needs building from scratch, what the agent actually needs to handle day-to-day. We map out the workflows, design the rate card structure, and work out where the gaps are in the data.

By the end of this, there's a clear blueprint: what gets built, how it connects, what you'll need to maintain. No guesswork going into the build.

Phase 2 Build it

Set up the data environment — Google Sheets or Airtable, kept as simple as the job requires. Build the automation workflow connecting a job input to the AI to a quote output. Test it against real historical jobs until the numbers are right.

Hand it over with enough documentation that it can be used, adjusted, and maintained without needing to call anyone. The goal is something you can actually run independently.
